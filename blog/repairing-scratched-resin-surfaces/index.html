Repairing Scratched Resin Surfaces

How to sand, fill, and polish surface scratches on cured epoxy and polyurethane resin projects

Sep 27, 2026

Repairing Scratched Resin Surfaces

Scratches on cured resin surfaces happen from contact during demolding, storage, or handling. Most superficial scratches remove by sanding and polishing, while deeper ones require filling before re-polishing. This guide covers the practical repair steps with specific grit sequences and material choices, including troubleshooting for common repair problems.

Assessing scratch depth

Run a fingertip across the surface to locate scratches, then examine each under overhead light at a shallow angle. Mark the deepest ones with a felt-tip pen. Superficial micro-scratches feel smooth and do not catch on the nail; deeper scratches catch and may be visible from above at an angle. Sort scratches into three categories: light (do not catch on fingertip), moderate (catch on fingertip but not deep), and deep (catch firmly on fingertip, visible as troughs).

Light scratches (do not catch on fingertip)

Dry sanding method

  1. Start with 400-grit sandpaper on a flat sanding block. Sand the scratched area in one direction only, using light even pressure. Progress through 600, 800, and 1000 grit, wiping the surface with 91% isopropyl alcohol between each grit to remove residue and inspect progress.

  2. At 1000 grit, if scratches remain, continue to 1500 grit. Many repair sequences stop at 1000 before polishing, but progressing to 1500 or 2000 produces a noticeably smoother finish with fewer visible transition marks.

  3. After the final grit, polish with a clean microfiber cloth and a small amount of automotive rubbing compound. Work in circular motions at low speed if using a machine polisher, or by hand with moderate pressure and firm circular strokes.

  4. Wipe clean with alcohol to inspect the result under overhead light. If any fine scratches remain, resume the grit progression from the last completed grit.

Wet sanding method (preferred for finer control and longer paper life)

  1. Soak 1000-grit wet/dry sandpaper in water for 10 minutes before use. Sand the scratched area with the paper lubricated with a mist of water, using gentle even strokes in one direction. Rinse the area and the paper frequently to keep the surface and abrasive clean.

  2. Progress to 1500 grit, then 2000 grit, keeping the surface wet throughout each stage. Each grit removes the scratches left by the previous one — if scratch patterns persist from the previous grit, do not progress until they are fully removed.

  3. At 2000 grit, the surface will feel glass-smooth to the touch. Rinse thoroughly and dry with a lint-free cloth, checking under overhead light for any remaining imperfections.

  4. Polish with a polishing compound and felt pad, or by hand with a microfiber cloth and a small amount of abrasive polish designed for clear coats. Hand polishing requires modest pressure and frequent wiping to inspect progress.

Moderate scratches (catch on fingertip but not deep)

Sand and fill method

  1. Sand the area with 400-grit paper on a flat block, removing the glossy finish and lightly beveling the scratch edges to create a small V-groove. This mechanical keying gives the filler something to grip. Progress through 600 and 800 grit, cleaning between each with alcohol.

  2. Mix a small batch of clear casting resin at the manufacturer’s specified ratio — typically 1:1 by volume for epoxy casting resin. For small scratch repairs, prepare 20–30ml total in a clean mixing cup, scraping sides and bottom during mixing.

  3. Apply the mixed resin into the scratched groove using a toothpick, small brush, or nylon applicator. Fill the groove slightly over the surface level, as casting resin shrinks approximately 2% by volume during cure, which can leave the repair below the surrounding surface if filled exactly to original level.

  4. Allow 24 hours at room temperature (70°F–75°F / 21°C–24°C) for full cure. The repair patch will be slightly proud of the surrounding surface once cured — this excess will be removed in the sanding stage.

  5. Sand the cured patch starting at 400 grit, progressing through 600, 800, 1000, 1500, and 2000 grit. Each grit must remove the scratches left by the previous grit completely before progressing. Use a sanding block for flatness throughout.

  6. Polish as described in the light-scratch method above, using a fresh polishing compound and clean pad or cloth.

Deep scratches (catch firmly on fingertip, visible as troughs)

Structural fill and re-coat method

  1. Grind the scratched area with 220-grit paper on a flat block, widening the existing scratch into a deliberate V-shaped groove approximately 2–3mm wide. This groove provides mechanical keying for the repair material. Clean all loose dust with compressed air, then wipe the area thoroughly with 91% isopropyl alcohol to remove any residual oils.

  2. Mix a batch of thickened repair resin: combine the mixed casting resin with colloidal silica (fumed silica) until the consistency resembles thick peanut butter. A typical ratio is 10 parts resin to 1 part silica by volume, but follow the silica manufacturer’s recommendations for load temperature. Mix small batches — thickened resin has a working life of approximately 15–20 minutes before it begins to gel.

  3. Press the thickened resin into the V-groove using a dental pick, palette knife, or stiff brush. Fill the groove completely and slightly overfill by approximately 0.5mm to account for shrinkage during cure. The overfill will be sanded flat in later steps.

  4. Allow minimum 24-hour cure at 70°F–75°F. Full hardness is typically reached at 72 hours. The filled area will be significantly proud of the surrounding cured surface — this is expected and will be removed by grinding.

  5. Grind the filled area flat starting at 220 grit, progressing through 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1500, and 2000 grit. Each grit removes the scratches from the previous one. Use a rigid sanding block for even material removal — sanding by hand on a curved surface creates low spots that are difficult to correct.

  6. Progress through final polishing stages as in the moderate-scratch method, starting again at 400 or 600 grit if the grind marks are deep, working back through the full sequence.

Troubleshooting repair problems

Problem Cause Fix
Swirl marks after polishing Polishing compound dried on surface before buffing, or excessive pressure applied during machine polishing Re-sand the area progressively through 1000 grit, then restart polishing with fresh compound at lower pressure, using slower circular motions
Repair patch visible after final polish Cure shrinkage pulled the patch below surrounding surface, or grit sequence was skipped, leaving transition scratches Grind the patch flat, verifying full cure (24+ hours at 70°F–75°F) before re-sanding; ensure each grit removes previous scratches 100% before progressing
Orange peel texture on repair surface Dust particles landed on wet resin during fill, or debris contaminated the mix during application Work in a cleaner environment; cover the repair area with a clean box or tent during the cure period; if contamination occurred, remove the flawed fill and remake the batch
Repair yellowed after weeks of cure Resin batch had excessive hardener ratio, or the repair was exposed to UV light through a window Use clear casting resin formulated for low yellowing (check datasheet for UV stabilizer content); for pieces near windows, apply a UV-resistant top coat after final polish
Repair feels soft or tacky after 48 hours Inaccurate mix ratio (too much or too little hardener), or moisture contamination during mixing The repair must fully cure before assessment — allow 72 hours at 70°F–75°F. If still soft, the mix ratio was incorrect; remake the repair with precisely measured components using a digital scale accurate to 0.1g

Bottom Line

Surface scratches on cured resin progressively remove by sanding through grits from 400 through 2000, then polishing with a compound. Deeper scratches require filling with thickened repair resin before sanding and polishing. Always verify full cure (24 hours minimum at 70°F–75°F, 72 hours for full hardness) before sanding a repair, and never skip grits in the progression — each grit must remove the scratches left by the previous one completely before advancing to the next. Work in a clean environment, use a sanding block for flatness, and verify each stage before progressing.

FAQ

Q: Can I use regular sandpaper from a hardware store on cured resin? A: Yes, but water-resistant wet/dry paper lasts longer and produces a finer finish if using the wet method. For dry sanding, any grit works, but mark the grit size on the paper back with a permanent marker if reusing pieces, as the numbers wear off with use.

Q: How many grit stages are necessary for a good result? A: At minimum, progress from your starting grit through 800, 1000, and 2000 before polishing. Skipping grits leaves visible transition scratches that require rework. The full sequence 400 → 600 → 800 → 1000 → 1500 → 2000 gives the smoothest, most professional result, especially on visible surfaces.

Q: Will the repair area be as clear as the original resin? A: A well-executed repair with clear casting resin and proper grit progression will be nearly indistinguishable from the original resin, especially from a normal viewing distance of 12–18 inches. Minor differences may appear under direct overhead light at a shallow angle, but these are typically only cosmetic.

Q: Can I repair scratches on colored or pigmented resin pieces? A: Yes, but you will need tinted repair resin matching the original color exactly. Mix small test batches (5–10ml) to match the hue before applying to the visible surface. For deep scratches on colored pieces, the fill may show a slight shade difference that becomes apparent under certain lighting conditions, so match carefully.

Q: How long after the original pour can I repair scratches on a resin piece? A: Scratches can be repaired at any time after the resin is fully cured, which is 7+ days for most epoxy systems. The mechanical bond between old and new resin layers depends on proper surface preparation — sanding the old surface and cleaning with 91% isopropyl alcohol before applying repair material. For pieces cured months or years ago, the surface may have accumulated dust or oils requiring more thorough cleaning.

Q: Is it better to wet sand or dry sand resin for scratch repair? A: Wet sanding produces a finer finish, lasts longer because the water prevents clogging, and reduces dust inhalation. However, dry sanding is faster for initial material removal and works well for very deep scratches where wet sanding would fill the grit pores. Many practitioners use wet sanding from 400 grit onward and reserve dry sanding only for the earliest material removal stages.
